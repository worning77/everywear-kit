The space, in two questions

We organize embodied AR interaction by two questions about any technique.

1 · Reference frame: what does the content bind to?

  • HEAD — the user's viewpoint (view-locked content, head-motion events)
  • +ARM — the forearm, wrist, or hand
  • +BODY — the torso or whole-body posture
  • +SCENE — an object, surface, or room in the world
  • +SOCIAL — another person

2 · Commitment: how much geometry does it hold?

  • Trigger — a discrete event fires something (nod, tap, posture change, zone entry)
  • Track — continuous motion maps to continuous output (pointing, dragging, rep counting)
  • Anchor — content is placed in the frame and must persist there over time and movement

The rules of thumb

  • Binding, not device. A ring, watch, or camera is a sensor, not a frame: a pinch detected by a ring and the same pinch detected by a camera are both +Arm.
  • Outermost frame. Pick the outermost frame the content genuinely recruits. Pointing your arm at a world target binds to the world (+Scene); tapping your own forearm binds to the arm (+Arm).
  • +Social means another person is essential: content binds to their body frame (a health bar above your opponent) or to a frame shared between users (a screen two people must see at the same place, at the same time). Content left in the world for others to find later, a Pokémon Go gym, needs no frame with a particular person: that is +Scene.
  • Primary commitment. Many techniques are composites (a track substrate with a trigger commit). Classify by what the user's act does to the content.
  • Ego-position. The head frame also carries the body's own place in the world: a walked trajectory, content pinned where you stood. Head-orientation content is drift-free; ego-position content rides the frontier.

The frontier

Body-bound frames (blue) are recoverable from on-body inertial sensors alone, no camera. World- and person-bound frames (purple) need an occasional external fix (a one-time scan, a shared start) because inertial position drifts. The design rule: place a demanding interaction at the simplest frame that holds it; spend the fix only when the frame truly requires it.

What we built (~3.5 min)

First one scenario that walks the hardest commitment, anchoring, across the frames; then five applications. All run live on glasses + watch + phone, no camera during use.

One scenario across the anchor row (61 s)

The same user carries content at every level of the anchor commitment: held at their own position by the glasses, on the wrist by the watch, on the body by the phone, pinned to the scanned room, and shared in a common frame with another person.

Indoor Navigator

Walking trigger reveals the panel; the floor path is anchored to the building by a one-time scan; rerouting when the elevator is out.

AR Gaming

Arm-direction aiming, arm-pose firing; the opponent's health bar rides a shared frame set by a common start.

Context-Aware System

A printer's status panel appears on approach; a grab copies progress to the wrist; a check-watch pose recalls it later.

Tele-Party

A shared world-anchored screen both users see in the same spot; standing pauses playback, sitting resumes it.

Pace Partner

A body-anchored exercise panel that stays put while running; a friend's recorded run replays as a ghost.
